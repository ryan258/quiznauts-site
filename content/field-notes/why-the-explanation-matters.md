A quiz can stop at a letter. B lights up, the score changes, and the next question arrives.

We want to leave a little more room than that.

A useful explanation tells you why the answer holds. It might separate two similar ideas, explain a surprising number, or place a familiar name in a clearer context. The aim is to give you something you can use after you have forgotten your score.

That changes how we write questions. We look for one clear thing to ask. We keep the choices parallel. We avoid double negatives, invented pass rates, and claims that a question will reveal how clever you are. If two answers could be defended, the wording needs work.

It also changes how we treat a miss. An incorrect choice can be understandable. The explanation should resolve the confusion without pretending we know what everyone else guessed or laughing at the person who chose it.

Sources belong close to the answer. They let you inspect the claim, find the missing context, or follow a question in a different direction. A source link is an invitation to look further; it does not turn a quiz score into a measure of education.

The little cow supplies the welcome. The editorial work has to earn the return visit.
