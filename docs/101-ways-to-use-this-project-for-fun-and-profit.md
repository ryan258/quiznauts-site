# 101 ways to use this project for fun and profit

Quiznauts already has the ingredients for a small creative workshop: Mr. Big the toy cow astronaut, an untimed three-question mission, sourced explanations, field notes, reusable artwork, and a local Hugo site. Use those ingredients to make something enjoyable, useful, or worth buying without turning the authoring tool into a business platform.

This is an idea menu, not a backlog or a publishing commitment. Pick one finished thing that fits the energy available. Fun is a sufficient outcome; earning money is an optional direction.

## Five recommended starting points

Start with **#21, #31, #41, #61, and #70, in that order**. These offer three quick ways to enjoy existing material, followed by two small experiments with a plausible path to paid work. The ranking reflects the project's current assets and Ryan's effort constraints; buyer demand is untested.

| Pick | Why start here | Smallest satisfying version | Main payoff |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **#21 — Make a question-and-reveal pair** | The ocean question and both graphics already exist. Assemble readable captions, the explanation, and the source. | One existing card pair with finished captions, saved together. | **Best overall starting point:** a finished piece to enjoy, reuse, or show as a sample. |
| **#31 — Write a mission-control exchange** | Mr. Big's earnest astronaut personality gives the scene an immediate comic setup. Dictate a short exchange around an existing question. | Six to ten lines between Mr. Big and mission control. | **Most creative fun for little effort**, while discovering his voice. |
| **#41 — Host a living-room quiz** | Use First Contact as it is. Playing with someone gives the project an immediate purpose and reveals which explanations start a conversation. | One person, three questions, answers spoken aloud. Let them operate the controls if that is easier. | **Fastest social reward**, with useful informal feedback. |
| **#61 — Offer a custom celebration quiz** | A birthday or reunion gives the quiz a specific recipient and purpose. Make a personal gift first to see whether the work is enjoyable. | One three-question private sample, plus a fixed package description: questions, answer sheet, and one revision. | **First choice for testing paid interest:** personalization gives a potential buyer a concrete reason to want it. |
| **#70 — Offer a question-quality review** | Apply fair wording, defensible answers, useful explanations, and sources. It needs less visual production than a new product. | A before-and-after review of three questions, showing exactly what improved and why. | **A second route to paid work**, especially if fixing questions is more enjoyable than designing graphics. |

**For the next session, choose #21.** Finish the existing ocean pair and its captions, then stop. That produces a complete result with little new research or production work.

For a money experiment, try **#61 before #70**: the finished object is more playful and personal. Keep the first sample small and see whether making it is enjoyable. An eventual offer could be: “A custom celebration quiz with an answer sheet and one revision.” Finding an interested buyer is a separate next step.

These ideas can share material. One reviewed question can become a card pair, a Mr. Big exchange, a spoken game, and a sample of editorial work. That lets effort accumulate into reusable assets. Choose one starting point; completing all five is optional.

## Play with what is here

1. **Take a three-question curiosity break.** **Use now.** Start `./qn`, play First Contact, and follow one source that catches your attention. Stop there if that is enough for today.

2. **Let someone else command the mission.** **Use now.** Read the choices aloud and let a friend answer with A, B, or C. The other person can operate the controls if that makes play easier.

3. **Run a cooperative expedition.** **Use now.** Have a small group agree on an answer before revealing it. Make the conversation the activity, with no individual leaderboard.

4. **Play confidence versus curiosity.** **Use now.** Before each reveal, say whether you are sure, guessing, or curious. Compare the feeling with the explanation without turning confidence into a public score.

5. **Try an explanation-first round.** **Use now.** Read an explanation aloud and ask someone to reconstruct the question it answers. It changes a familiar mission into a listening game.

6. **Play the no-JavaScript edition.** **Use now.** Use the mission's existing reveal path without JavaScript as a slower read-and-reveal activity. This is another way to enjoy the content, not an accessibility certification.

7. **Give your expedition a ridiculous name.** **Use now.** Invent a crew name and a one-sentence captain's log after playing. Keep it in a local note rather than building a player account system.

8. **Choose the best wrong answer.** **Use now.** Discuss which distractor was most tempting and why. The result can reveal an interesting distinction to explain more clearly.

9. **Follow a source rabbit hole.** **Use now.** Open a source linked from First Contact and find one detail beyond the quiz answer. Save the link and a sentence about what surprised you.

10. **Stage Mr. Big's mission briefing.** **Make.** Write a short welcome and read it in a solemn mission-control voice. A toy cow taking three questions extremely seriously supplies the joke.

## Invent small missions

11. **Make a kitchen-table science mission.** **Make.** Research three questions about everyday materials or household observations. Keep it a quiz rather than an experiment requiring equipment or risky demonstrations.

12. **Make a backyard nature mission.** **Make.** Pick three plants, animals, or natural processes from one place. Use sources that distinguish the actual species and location.

13. **Build a local-history postcard.** **Make.** Research three facts about a town or neighborhood and pair them with a short introduction. Preserve archive or museum references alongside the answers.

14. **Create a movie-craft mission.** **Make.** Ask about editing, sound, practical effects, or film terminology. Write original questions and use your own visual treatment.

15. **Explore a word's journey.** **Make.** Build a three-question mission around word origins or changing meanings. Give each answer a dictionary or historical-language source.

16. **Make a two-things-people-confuse mission.** **Make.** Choose one distinction, such as rotation versus a sunrise-to-sunrise cycle. Use three questions to explore it from different angles.

17. **Create a museum-object expedition.** **Make.** Choose three objects documented in an institution's collection catalog. Link the object records and use original descriptions; any reproduced images need their own permission check.

18. **Make a scale-and-distance mission.** **Make.** Ask about relative sizes, durations, or distances in one topic. Explain the comparison rather than relying on a surprising number alone.

19. **Build a birthday mission.** **Make.** Write a private three-question quiz about a friend's interests, with their preferences in mind. Keep personal details out of public content unless they want them shared.

20. **Create a fictional-universe expedition.** **Make.** Write original lore about an imaginary destination and quiz the reader on it. Label the premise as fiction and keep it separate from sourced real-world facts.

## Turn one question into several good pieces

21. **Make a question-and-reveal pair.** **Use now.** Start with the existing ocean question and explanation cards. Prepare readable captions with the question, choices, answer, and source.

22. **Expand one explanation into a field note.** **Make.** Take the useful distinction behind a question and explain it in a few paragraphs. Link back to the mission and its primary source.

23. **Write a “why the other choices fail” companion.** **Make.** Explain what makes each distractor plausible and what rules it out. Avoid inventing claims about what most people answer.

24. **Record a short spoken quiz.** **Make.** Read the prompt, leave an untimed pause the listener can control, then give the explanation. Save a transcript with the audio.

25. **Make a vertical quiz clip.** **Make.** Turn one reviewed question into a recorded question-and-reveal sequence using the vertical artwork as a starting composition. Video production and captioning are separate work.

26. **Build a three-slide mini lesson.** **Make.** Use a question, a visual explanation, and a source slide. Export a separate presentation or images; the site does not generate slides automatically.

27. **Write a “one more question” ending.** **Make.** Finish a field note with a researched follow-up question. Give the reader a reason to keep exploring without promising the next installment.

28. **Make a readable plain-text edition.** **Make.** Put a mission's prompts, choices, separated answers, explanations, and sources into one text file. It can travel without the website or artwork.

29. **Create a caption bank from one mission.** **Make.** Draft a curious, a playful, and a straightforward introduction to the same material. Save the alternatives locally for later use.

30. **Assemble one complete content bundle.** **Make.** Package a mission, a field note, paired graphics, captions, and a source list. One finished bundle can supply several future uses without a new content stream.

## Keep the project enjoyable enough to return to

101. **Make one thing, save it, and leave yourself a return path.** **Use now.** Pick any idea above, define the smallest finished version, and do that version. Save a short next-action note and use `./qn backup` when you want a verified local source checkpoint; a copy elsewhere can protect against losing the disk.

## Keep each experiment small

Before taking on paid work, define the recipient, deliverable, review limit, delivery method, and permission to use the material. Use a finite sample to learn whether someone wants it. New accounts, purchases, external messages, publishing, and payment services remain separate choices; writing this list does not activate them.

For a short local session: run `./qn status`, choose one idea number, and open `./qn` if a preview helps. Dictate rough copy if that is easier. End with the finished file or a single clear next action, so returning does not require reconstructing the whole plan.
